lesson 01 of 03

options basics: what you hold

four kinds of equity show up in startup offers. they differ in who can get them, what you pay, and when the tax bill arrives.

the four kinds

an option is the right to buy a share later at a fixed price, the strike price, set on the day of the grant. you pay nothing until you exercise. if the share is worth more than the strike, the gap is called the spread.

iso (incentive stock option, US) is an option only employees can get. there is no regular income tax when you exercise, though the spread counts toward the alternative minimum tax. if you hold the shares two years from grant and one year from exercise, the whole gain is taxed as long-term capital gain. only options worth up to one hundred thousand dollars, measured at grant, can first become exercisable in any calendar year; anything above that is treated as an nso. you usually have to exercise within three months of leaving to keep iso treatment.

nso (non-qualified stock option, US) can go to employees, advisors and contractors. the spread at exercise is taxed as ordinary income.

rsu (restricted stock unit) is a promise of shares with no strike price. you pay nothing, and the full value is taxed as ordinary income when the units vest and are delivered.

esop in India is the usual word for an option under an employee stock option scheme. when you exercise, the gap between fair market value and your exercise price is a perquisite, taxed as salary at your slab rate. when you later sell, the gain above that fair market value is a capital gain. employees of eligible startups can defer the tax on exercise until the earliest of forty-eight months from the end of the assessment year, leaving the company, or selling.

worked example, us

ten thousand options, strike one dollar, share worth five dollars on the day you exercise, later sold at eight dollars.

typeat exercise or vestat sale
nso$40,000 ordinary income$30,000 capital gain
iso, held long enough$0 regular tax, $40,000 amt adjustment$70,000 long-term gain
rsu (no strike)$50,000 ordinary income$30,000 capital gain

worked example, india

one thousand esops, exercise price ten rupees, fair market value four hundred rupees on exercise, later sold at nine hundred rupees.

recall

you get sixty thousand isos at a ten dollar strike, vesting evenly over four years. how many of each year's options are treated as nsos?

here's the reasoning. each year fifteen thousand options become exercisable, worth one hundred and fifty thousand dollars at the grant price. only one hundred thousand dollars of that fits under the iso limit, so five thousand options a year are treated as nsos.

in India, when is an esop first taxed: at grant, at vesting, at exercise or at sale?

here's the reasoning. grant and vesting give you only a right. the first tax point is exercise, when the gap between fair market value and your exercise price becomes a salary perquisite. sale is the second tax point, for the gain above that fair market value. eligible startup employees can defer the first payment.

lesson 02 of 03

vesting, cliffs and triggers

a grant is a promise that becomes yours in pieces. the schedule decides when, and the triggers decide what happens if the company is sold.

the pieces

vesting is the schedule on which your grant becomes yours. the common one is four years, monthly.

the cliff is a waiting period at the start. nothing vests until it passes, then everything that would have vested so far arrives at once. a one-year cliff is common in the US. in India it is close to the rule: the companies share capital rules require at least one year between grant and vesting.

single trigger acceleration means some unvested shares vest when the company is acquired. double trigger needs two events: the acquisition, and then you losing your job without cause (or quitting for good reason) within a set window, often twelve months. double trigger is far more common, because buyers want the team to stay.

early exercise and 83(b): some US companies let you exercise before vesting. you then own restricted stock, and you can file an 83(b) election with the IRS within thirty days to be taxed on today's spread, often zero, instead of on the value as it vests. the thirty days do not stretch.

worked example

forty-eight thousand options, granted 01.04.26, four years monthly, one-year cliff.

recall

your grant has a one-year cliff and you leave after eleven months. what have you vested?

here's the reasoning. nothing. the cliff is all or nothing: the first year's shares arrive only on the cliff date. if you are close to it, the date is worth knowing before you resign.

the company is acquired and you keep your job. with double trigger acceleration, does anything speed up?

here's the reasoning. no. only the first trigger has fired. acceleration waits for the second one, a termination without cause or a good-reason exit inside the window the plan sets.

lesson 03 of 03

409a versus preferred

investors buy one kind of share and employees get another. the two prices are different on purpose, and the headline number belongs to the investors.

two prices, two kinds of share

preferred shares are what investors buy in a round. they come with rights you do not have, above all a liquidation preference: in a sale they get their money back before common holders see anything.

common shares are what your options turn into. because they sit behind the preference, they are worth less than preferred.

the 409a valuation is the US appraisal of common stock that sets your strike price. a strike below fair market value can trigger penalty tax under section 409A, so companies use a safe harbour method, most often an independent appraisal no older than twelve months. that is why it is refreshed after each round.

in India the fair market value of unlisted shares for esop tax comes from a category one merchant banker under rule 3(8). many Indian startups set the exercise price at the face value of the share, often ten rupees or one rupee, which makes the perquisite at exercise nearly the whole fair market value.

worked example: the headline price versus your payout

ten million shares fully diluted. investors put in ten million dollars for two and a half million preferred shares, at four dollars each, with a one times non-participating preference. the company later sells for fifteen million dollars.

linemathamount
headline price per share$15,000,000 ÷ 10,000,000$1.50
investors, converted to common25% × $15,000,000$3,750,000
investors, taking the preference1 × $10,000,000$10,000,000
left for common$15,000,000 − $10,000,000$5,000,000
per common share$5,000,000 ÷ 7,500,000$0.67

investors take the larger of the two, the preference. your share gets sixty-seven cents, not a dollar fifty, and not the four dollars they paid.

recall

a recruiter says your twenty thousand options are "worth eighty thousand dollars at the last round price of four dollars". what does that number leave out?

here's the reasoning. four dollars is the preferred price, which buys a liquidation preference you do not get. your options also cost the strike to exercise. a fairer number is (a realistic exit price per common share minus your strike) times twenty thousand, and the 409a is a better floor than the preferred price.

why does a US company refresh its 409a after raising a round?

here's the reasoning. a round is new information about value, so the old appraisal may no longer be reasonable. granting options below fair market value risks 409A penalty tax for the employee, and the safe harbour only holds for an appraisal no older than twelve months with nothing material changed since.

  2. 26 CFR § 1.409A-1(b)(5)(iv)(B), safe harbour valuation methods
  3. IRS, topic 427: stock options
  4. income tax department of India, taxation of esops (rule 3(8) valuation)
